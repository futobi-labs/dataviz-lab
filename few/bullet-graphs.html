Bullet graphs

Few designed the bullet graph in 2005 to do a gauge's job in a fraction of the space: one measure, one target, a few qualitative ranges, on a straight scale the eye can actually read. Here it is to spec, six KPIs stacked on a shared layout, each on its own scale.

Six KPIs, year to date through October

Each row has its own scale because the units differ. All scales start at zero.

The same six KPIs, as the space a gauge would take

Footprints drawn to one scale. The gauges are outlines only: a typical BI dial at 180 by 170 px including its label and readout, against a compact bullet graph at 300 by 40 px including its label.

Notes

  • This follows Few's Bullet Graph Design Specification: two to five qualitative ranges in intensities of one hue (three grays here, darkest for poor), the featured measure as a bar one third the band height, the comparative measure as a perpendicular tick, and a quantitative scale that starts at zero because the bar encodes length.
  • Stacking works because every row shares the same geometry: label column, band height, origin. The eye compares each bar to its own target and bands, not to its neighbors. Sorting by value is meaningless across mixed units, so the order is fixed and logical instead.
  • Information Dashboard Design spends pages on why gauges fail: the circle wastes the corners, the arc is harder to judge than a line, and most products omit the target. The footprint figure is the argument in pixels.
  • Arguable: the qualitative bands are someone's opinion baked into the chart. If "good" is not agreed, the gray bands mislead more quietly than a red light would, and a plain bar with a target tick is the more honest graph.
  • The vertical variant is in the spec and useful in a row of columns, but horizontal wins whenever labels are words: they read left to right next to the scale.

Data: synthetic, generated in-page with Lab.rng(42) (few/few.js). Gauge footprint is a stated assumption, not a measurement of any product.
