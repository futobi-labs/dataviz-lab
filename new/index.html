New data viz

Few and Tufte wrote mostly for the printed page and the single screen. These pieces are about what the page could not do: change state as you read, show a distribution as outcomes instead of a summary, and let one chart interrogate another.

One cohort, six views

Scrollytelling with a sticky unit chart. A thousand applicants regroup six times, and the aggregate hire rate turns out to be hiding a screening bias.

Uncertainty you can feel

Hypothetical outcome plots against error bars, a quantile dotplot with a draggable deadline, and a fan chart against its own sample paths.

Linked views

Brushing and linking across four coordinated charts of 2,000 rides. Crossfilter semantics in plain JavaScript, a canvas scatter and a live count.

What "new" means here

Not novelty. Each technique below has a research lineage of ten to forty years; what changed is that a browser can now run them at sixty frames a second on a phone. The bar for using them is the same one Few and Tufte set for ink: each must show something a static chart cannot.

Interaction as a question. A brush or a filter should let the reader ask the next question, not decorate the answer to the last one.

Uncertainty as outcomes. People reason better about frequencies and draws than about intervals and densities. Show the outcomes.

Narrative as sequence. Order is an argument. A scroll-driven story is a chosen path through the data, and should say so.

Motion with a job. Animation carries identity between states or samples from a distribution. If it only entertains, cut it. Every piece honors reduced-motion settings.
