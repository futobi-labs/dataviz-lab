One cohort, six views

A thousand fictional applicants, one dot each, carried through six arrangements by scrolling. The dots never get swapped out, so every regrouping is the same people seen a different way. The argument builds the way an analyst's notebook does: the aggregate first, then the confound, then the comparison that settles it.

Notes

  • Object constancy is the whole trick. Bostock's 2012 essay of that name makes the case: when the same mark persists across views, the eye tracks people, not abstractions. Swap the dots for fresh ones between steps and the story turns into six unrelated charts.
  • Scroll triggers, not scroll-jacking. Steps fire with an IntersectionObserver when they cross the middle of the viewport, the pattern from Bostock's "How To Scroll" and Russell Goldenberg's Scrollama. The page never hijacks the wheel, and the buttons and arrow keys reach every state without scrolling.
  • This is an author-driven, martini-glass structure (Segel and Heer, 2010): a fixed narrow path, then the open table below for anyone who wants to check. The reader cannot ask the next question, and that is a real cost.
  • The band is a choice. Seventy to 84 is where the channels overlap enough to compare. A different band moves the ratio. A story format makes cherry-picking that choice easy and invisible, so say which band, and why, every time.
  • Earn the scroll. Archie Tse's rule at the Times was that anything beyond plain scrolling has to justify itself. Six states is about the ceiling before a static small-multiple would serve the reader better.

Source: synthetic. 1,000 fictional applicants generated in-page from a seeded random source (seed 11). Channel shares, assessment scores and a two-stage logistic screen with a built-in referral bonus are invented for illustration.
