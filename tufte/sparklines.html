Sparklines

Data-intense, design-simple, word-sized graphics. A sparkline earns its place by sitting where the number would have gone, so the reader gets the history without leaving the sentence or the row.

In running text

Over the last 80 readings the patient's blood glucose stayed inside the normal range on of them; every excursion runs upward, peaking at mg/dL, and the low was . Harbor Instruments finished the year from its first close, but the path ran through a drawdown of , which a single year-over-year number would never show. Port Avalon's daily high traced the expected year, from in winter to in summer, while its monthly rainfall was heaviest in .

Red marks the latest value, which is also the number printed after each line.

Blue marks the minimum and maximum.

The gray band behind the glucose line is the normal range, 70 to 120 mg/dL. It does the job of an axis: it answers "is this fine?" without a single tick.

Each sparkline is sized in em. Zoom the page and the graphics grow with the type. Hover any of them for the value under the pointer.

Own range stretches every line to fill its row. Shape is maximally legible; magnitude is gone. A 4 percent wobble and a 60 percent collapse look equally dramatic.

Common, indexed rebases each series to 100 at the first session and puts all twelve on one logarithmic scale, so a doubling and a halving are the same height. The faint hairline is 100. Quiet rows now look quiet, and the one big winner no longer gets to flatten everyone else.

Neither is wrong. The row's printed numbers carry magnitude either way, which is why a sparkline should never travel without them.

Win and loss

An 82-game season, six fictional clubs

One tick per game in schedule order: up for a win, down for a loss. Sorted by wins.

Binary outcomes compress to their essence: direction only. Streaks, slumps and the second-half collapse become shapes.

Blue picks out each club's longest winning streak. Losses are drawn lighter than wins so the eye reads the up-strokes first.

Tufte's version used a baseball season; the form works for any yes-or-no series, from builds passing to on-time deliveries.

Notes

  • The definition is from Beautiful Evidence: "data-intense, design-simple, word-sized graphics." The context, a sentence or a table row, supplies the labels, so the graphic needs none.
  • At word size the height is fixed by the type, which means choosing the width is choosing the slope. Cleveland's banking to 45 degrees matters more here than in a full chart, and most dashboard sparklines are far too wide and flat.
  • Per-row scaling is the single most common way sparklines mislead in business dashboards. If the question is "which moved most," use a common scale; if it is "what shape did each take," use own ranges and print the numbers.
  • Pushback: sparklines in flowing prose are beautiful in print and fragile on screens. Reflow can strand a graphic at a line break, and screen readers need the aria label to say what the eye sees. In practice they belong in tables far more often than in paragraphs.

Source: all series are synthetic and fictional, generated in-page with a seeded random number generator (Lab.rng, seeds 1983 and up). Patient, securities, city and clubs are invented.
