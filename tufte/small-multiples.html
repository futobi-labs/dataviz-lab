Small multiples

The same design repeated across slices of the data, on the same scales, close enough together that the eye compares without remembering. Once the reader has decoded one panel, every other panel is free.

Anscombe's quartet

Four datasets, one set of summary statistics

Range-frames. Each axis line runs only from that panel's minimum to its maximum, and the end ticks are labeled with the exact extremes. The frame itself is data: panel IV's horizontal frame starting at 8 says something the other three do not.

All four panels share both scales, so the frames can be compared directly.

The blue line is the least-squares fit. It is the same line in every panel, which is precisely the problem.

Twenty-four climates

Mean daily high by month, 24 fictional cities

Sort Scale

The faint horizontal line in each panel is the mean across all 24 cities, the same height everywhere. It turns each panel into an answer to "compared to what?"

warmest month, coldest month.

The curves that run upside down are southern-hemisphere cities. Nobody has to point them out; a shared scale and a shared design make the exception announce itself.

Switch to each its own scale and every city looks equally seasonal. The near-equatorial cities, a few degrees of swing, now wave as hard as the continental ones. That is the lie that free scales tell by default.

The same cities as a dot-dash plot

Annual mean against seasonal swing

A scatterplot whose axes are its marginal distributions: each dash on the frame is one city. Hover a point or a panel above; they are linked.

The dot-dash plot replaces each axis with a rug of the one-dimensional data. Clusters, gaps and outliers in each variable are visible on the frame before you look at the joint pattern.

northern hemisphere, southern. Two marks, no legend box.

Notes

  • "At the heart of quantitative reasoning is a single question: Compared to what?" Small multiples answer it structurally (Envisioning Information, chapter 4). The comparisons happen within the eyespan, without paging, toggling or memory.
  • Range-frames and dot-dash plots come from the chapter on multifunctioning graphical elements in The Visual Display of Quantitative Information: erase the non-data ink of the frame, then make what remains carry data. Anscombe's quartet opens the same book as the argument for graphing at all.
  • Ordering is half the design. Alphabetical, the default in nearly every BI tool, is the order least likely to reveal anything. Sort by a quantity the reader cares about, and the grid becomes a ranked display as well as a set of portraits.
  • Pushback: a shared scale can flatten low-amplitude panels into lines, and sometimes those small wiggles are the point. A second view with free scales is legitimate, but it must be labeled loudly and never be the only view.
  • The range-frame's minimum and maximum labels invite exact reading, which a conventional axis discourages. For noisy data that precision can be spurious; round accordingly.

Source: Anscombe's quartet from F. J. Anscombe, "Graphs in Statistical Analysis," The American Statistician 27(1), 1973, pp. 17 to 21, reproduced exactly; summary statistics computed in-page. City temperatures are synthetic and fictional, generated with a seeded random number generator (Lab.rng, seed 2024).
