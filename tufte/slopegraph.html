Slopegraph

Fifteen departments, two fiscal years, thirty printed numbers. The slopegraph is a table that has been allowed to draw: every value is legible, and the angle of each line is the change.

Port Avalon general fund, share of spending by department

Percent of the general fund, FY2016 and FY2026. Hover or tap a line to isolate it.

Position by

Labels that would collide are nudged apart by iterative relaxation; a hairline leader joins any label that had to move to the true end of its line. The lines never move, only the words.

Ranks spaces the rows evenly, which ends collisions and turns every crossing into a visible swap, at the cost of magnitude: a 0.1 point exchange looks the same as a 4 point surge.

Notes

  • The form first appears in The Visual Display of Quantitative Information as a table-graphic of government receipts as a share of GDP for fifteen countries, 1970 and 1979. Tufte named it the slopegraph later. Fifteen rows here is a nod to that original.
  • It works because it is redundant in the right way: the numbers serve lookup, the slopes serve comparison, the crossings serve rank change. Three readings, one set of marks, no legend.
  • These are shares of a fixed whole. Every point gained by pensions and debt service was taken from somewhere, so the slopes are not independent and should not be read as fifteen separate stories.
  • Pushback: two endpoints hide the path. A share that spiked in FY2020 and came back reads as flat. When the middle years matter, use small multiples of the full series instead.
  • In value mode the label crowding at the bottom is real information, it says the small departments are close together, but it also costs legibility. Relaxation trades vertical accuracy of the labels for readability; the leaders keep that trade honest.

Source: fictional figures written by hand for this page; Port Avalon is an invented city. Each year's fifteen shares sum to 100.0 percent.
