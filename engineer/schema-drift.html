The engineer's eye

Schema drift

A schema is a contract right up until the source system decides otherwise. Most of it was additive and harmless. It still moved through the layers at the speed of a sprint board, and the one change nobody announced cost two nights of loads.

Notes

  • Laid out by table, drift reads as weather: steady, mostly upward, mostly boring. The interesting structure is diagonal. The same column steps up in a Bronze lane, then a Silver lane, then a Gold lane weeks later, and the paired red ticks under the rename are its blast radius: four tables downstream, two nights in a row.
  • Column count is a blunt instrument. A rename or a widened decimal leaves the line flat, which is why they get letters instead of steps, and some of the worst drift never shows here at all: a column that keeps its name and type but changes meaning, a nullable field that starts arriving null, a code list that gains a value.
  • The additions were never the problem; a permissive Bronze swallows them. The rename was a contract break, and the load was the first thing to notice. A contract test at the source boundary, run before the copy, turns that R glyph into a failed check with a named owner instead of a failed night.
  • Pushback on the second chart: lag is not a defect. Two weeks per layer is a release cadence, and a column that shows up in Gold the same day it lands in Bronze is usually one nobody tested. Measure it to plan around it, not to shrink it.
