The engineer's eye

The engineer's eye

Data engineers spend their nights inside load logs, lineage graphs, star schemas and test results, and almost all of it gets drawn as a red-or-green table. These pages draw it the way a visualization person would: one warehouse, one year of nightly loads, five ways of looking.

Computed in-page from the shared model below.

The nightly heartbeat

A year of Gold finish times in one strip, and any night opened up task by task: what waited on a source, a dependency or a free slot, and which chain set the finish.

Lineage as a transit map

Source systems as lines, tables as stations, conformed tables as interchanges. Then a single Gold column traced back to the Bronze columns it came from.

Star schemas, taken literally

Facts as constellations, conformed dimensions as the stars they share. Beside it, the Kimball bus matrix, which says the same thing more usefully.

A year of tests

Every data-quality result on a calendar. The weekly rhythm, the incidents, and the month-end test that cries wolf twelve times a year.

Schema drift

Every table's columns over the year, and how long a new column takes to travel from Bronze to Gold.

The warehouse

A fictional distributor running a medallion warehouse of the kind you would build in Fabric: six source systems landing nightly, cleaned and conformed, then modeled into a star schema. The year is simulated, not drawn by hand. A scheduler with four slots runs each table when its source has arrived and its dependencies have finished, so every wait, cascade and late night follows from the rules.

Bronze. Raw landings from ERP, CRM, warehouse management, web events, the general ledger and HR. Append-only, typed as the source sends it.

Silver. Cleaned, typed and conformed. Customers merged across ERP and CRM; currency converted; sessions built from events.

Gold. Seven dimensions and seven facts. The layer the business reads, and the one with a 06:00 deadline.

Six stories are planted in the year and left for the pictures to find: Black Friday, an unannounced ERP rename, a spring of orphaned CRM accounts, a fortnight of throttled capacity, a bot, and a CRM vendor that cut its API limits.
